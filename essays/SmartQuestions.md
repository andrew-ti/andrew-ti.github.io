An important skill for software engineers is the ability to ask good questions. When a developer runs into a problem, writing their question well can be the difference between finding the solution to their problem within a few hours or making a thread that goes untouched for years. Eric Raymond's How to Ask Questions the Smart Way gives several guidelines for doing this: research the problem first, explain your goal, show what you’ve already tried, and provide relevant technical information. A good question should provide any would-be answerers with enough information to understand the problem without having to do any guesswork.

A good example of this is this question ([https://stackoverflow.com/questions/80002351/how-to-copy-development-branches-from-git-repository-to-another](url)): the author clearly explains that they are combining two repositories and shows the commands they’ve already used while also explaining what has worked and what the remaining problems are, providing ample information for others to be able to help.

To contrast, we have this question ([https://stackoverflow.com/questions/52809971/control-wordpad-through-excel-vba](url)): while some information was provided, several pieces of the puzzle are missing, including what the asker has already tried and exactly what they are trying to accomplish. One of the responses has to ask them what they have tried so far, creating an unnecessary back-and-forth that is inconvenient for all parties involved.

Asking for help is a much more involved process than merely saying that something is broken. I should first try to understand the problem myself, explain what I’m trying to accomplish, and show what I’ve already tried to make it as easy as possible for anyone who may be willing to help me. Providing relevant code and error messages is also important when they are available. These principles apply beyond Stack Overflow as well—anywhere that assistance would be needed, it’s best to be as informative as possible when asking for help.
